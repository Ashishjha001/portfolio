Every founder asks some version of "what percentage of revenue should we spend on marketing?" The honest answer has three parts: what peers actually spend, what your economics allow, and how the allocation should shift by stage. In that order: because the benchmark without the ceiling produces a number that cannot be defended.

## What private SaaS companies actually spend

The best public source is SaaS Capital's annual survey: private, B2B SaaS companies only, more than 1,000 respondents, completed March 2026. Their published medians as a percent of ARR:

| Department                   | Median spend, % of ARR | Change vs prior year |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------- | -------------------- |
| Research and development     | 22%                    | unchanged            |
| Selling costs                | 15%                    | up from 13%          |
| General and administrative   | 15%                    | up from 14%          |
| Customer support and success | 9%                     | up from 8%           |
| **Marketing**                | **8%**                 | **unchanged**        |
| Hosting                      | 5%                     | unchanged            |

The same survey draws the funding split plainly: bootstrapped companies spend a total median of 96% of ARR and 83% of them operate at or within two points of breakeven; equity-backed companies spend 101% of ARR and 48% operate at a loss. Equity-backed companies spend **100% more on marketing** than bootstrapped ones: a deliberate growth trade, not an inefficiency. Median growth: 20% annually for bootstrapped, 25% for equity-backed.

Two honest readings follow. First, if you are bootstrapped, the 8% median is the center of your peer distribution: spending meaningfully above it should be a deliberate, documented choice. Second, if you are equity-backed, expect your investors to push you above the median; that is what the funding is for. The number that is wrong for both is one copied without a reason.

## Stage-of-company ranges, and their limits

Practitioner-published ranges (vendor content, not survey data, treat as context) commonly describe seed-stage SaaS spending 15 to 25 percent and scaling toward the 8 to 12 percent median at later stages. No single public dataset cleanly segments marketing spend by ARR stage the way SaaS Capital segments by funding type, anyone quoting precise stage tables is usually extrapolating. If your board needs stage-segmented numbers, the SaaS Capital summary report is the primary source to buy; the funding-type split above is the honest free cut.

## The step the benchmark articles skip: compute your ceiling first

A percentage of ARR tells you what peers spend. It does not tell you what a customer is worth or what you can pay to acquire one. That derivation lives in the [break-even CAC worksheet](/insights/break-even-cac-worksheet/): contribution per customer, divided by the payback tolerance the business accepts, gives the maximum fully loaded CAC; the close rate converts that to a maximum cost per qualified lead. Every channel target in the budget inherits from that line.

The two numbers then check each other. If your ceiling implies a budget below the 8% median, the honest budget is the ceiling: the median does not pay your bills. If your ceiling implies 20% and you are bootstrapped, you can spend it, but write down why you are spending like an equity-backed company without the equity. That sentence is the difference between a budget and a hope.

## Allocation by motion, not by habit

Once the total is set, the split depends on how you sell:

- **Sales-led (high ACV, demos):** the SaaS Capital selling-cost median of 15% versus marketing's 8% is the shape of this motion: sales carries the pipeline, marketing creates the shortlist. Content, SEO and AI-search visibility compound here because the buying committee researches before it ever talks to you.
- **Product-led (self-serve, low ACV):** the split inverts, "marketing" partially merges into product and growth engineering; free tools, in-product loops and lifecycle emails replace outbound.
- **Hybrid:** most private SaaS companies live here; the allocation question is really "what does the funnel leak first?", run the [funnel leakage check](/tools/) before moving dollars.

For the regional cut, the [India demand generation budget guide](/insights/b2b-saas-demand-generation-budget-india/) covers how the same percentages translate in the Indian market's salary and media-cost structure.

## What the benchmark cannot tell you

The survey is private-company self-reported, US-weighted, and a snapshot of one quarter. It cannot tell you whether your category's CPCs make paid viable, whether your sales cycle can even use the budget this year, or whether your CAC payback tolerance is 12 months or 24: that is a board decision, not a benchmark. And no percentage survives first contact with a leaky funnel: spend the first budget tranche on knowing where the funnel leaks, not on filling it.

## The decision sequence, stated once more

1. Compute the ceiling: worksheet, five inputs, thirty minutes with finance.
2. Read the benchmark: 8% median, funding split, per-department context.
3. Set the total: ceiling constrained, benchmark contextualized, deviation written down.
4. Allocate by motion: sales-led, product-led or hybrid, checked against the funnel.
5. Review quarterly against CAC and pipeline contribution: not annually against the percentage.
